# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Moore, F., Haqiqi, I., Kong, Q., Rennels, L., Baldos, U., Ganapathi, H., Huber, M., & Hertel, T.** (2026). *New labour and agricultural damages improve climate cost estimates*. **Nature Climate Change**. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02749-z

  **Summary:** This study builds the first economy-wide estimate of how heat stress erodes human labor productivity worldwide and folds those losses into the social cost of carbon, the standard metric used to price greenhouse gas emissions in policy. Combining high-resolution climate projections with global economic modeling, the authors find that heat-driven lost labor is the second-largest contributor to the social cost of carbon after heat-related mortality, with each tonne of CO2 emitted in 2025 causing roughly $41 in lost productivity concentrated in hot, populous regions such as India, Nigeria, China, Pakistan, the Middle East, and sub-Saharan Africa. Combined with updated agricultural damage estimates, this labor-productivity accounting pushes the overall social cost of carbon toward roughly $200 per tonne, closely matching the U.S. EPA's 2023 estimate and well above values used in many current policies. The findings matter because they show that a routinely overlooked climate damage channel — the physical inability to work safely and efficiently in extreme heat — is large enough to meaningfully raise the price the world should pay to avoid emitting carbon, with the heaviest burden falling on lower-income, high-heat-exposure countries that contributed the least to causing the problem.
