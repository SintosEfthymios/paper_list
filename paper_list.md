# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Merfort, L., Popp, A., Bauer, N., Humpenöder, F., Abrahão, G., Bodirsky, B. L., Chen, D. M.-C., Köhler-Schindler, L., Luderer, G., Mandaroux, R., Merfort, A., Strefler, J., & Kriegler, E.** (2026). *Cost-effective climate change mitigation through land sector contributions*. **Nature Sustainability**. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-026-01977-7

  **Summary:** Using the coupled REMIND–MAgPIE energy–economy and land-use model, this study tests how different packages of agriculture, forestry and other land-use (AFOLU) policies — such as halting deforestation, reforestation, peatland rewetting and cutting agricultural methane and nitrous oxide — change the economic cost of meeting Paris-aligned temperature targets. The authors find that land-based measures substantially lower the cost of mitigation: with the full set of land measures in place, the modelled target can be reached with a carbon price of around US$130 and a consumption loss of about 1.3%, whereas leaving the land sector out shifts the burden to the energy sector, raises costs sharply and puts 1.5°C out of reach this century. The study also cautions that much of this land potential lies in the Global South, where food security, water scarcity and biodiversity pressures limit what can be done. The results matter because they show that land-use policy is an economic lever for climate policy, not just an add-on — but one that has to be governed carefully to avoid harmful trade-offs.
