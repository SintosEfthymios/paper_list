# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Tan, B. Y., Li, L., Kjellstrom, T., Otto, M., Lemke, B., Cai, W., Zhao, M., & Lee, J. K. W.** (2026). *Climate Change Projected to Worsen Global Economic Inequality Due To Lost Worker Productivity*. **GeoHealth**, 10(3), e2026GH001815. https://doi.org/10.1029/2026GH001815

  **Summary:** This study asks whether heat-driven losses in worker productivity will widen the economic gap between rich and poor nations, since both high- and low-income countries sit in the hot, humid tropics where heat stress hits hardest. Combining epidemiological heat-productivity relationships with climate projections, the authors model how rising temperatures will erode work capacity across sectors and countries as warming continues. They find that heat-related productivity losses, especially in outdoor, labor-intensive sectors like agriculture, fall disproportionately on lower-income countries that depend heavily on such work, even though the raw temperature exposure is similar across income groups. This means climate change acts as an inequality multiplier: it does not just shrink the global economic pie, it redistributes losses toward the countries least able to absorb them. The findings matter for climate adaptation and international development policy, underscoring the need for targeted worker-protection and adaptation investment in heat-exposed, lower-income economies as global temperatures rise.
