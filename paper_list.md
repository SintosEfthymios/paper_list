# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Polasky, S., Hawthorne, P. L., Chaplin-Kramer, R., Smith, J., Gerber, J. S., Mamun, S., Ruckelshaus, M., Russ, J., Schmitt, R., Vogl, A. L., Castonguay, A. C., Douglass, J., Kowal, V., Madden, I., Sharp, R., Sohngen, B., Chang, J., Daily, G., Heger, M. P., Holden, M., Johnson, J., Mandle, L., McDonald-Madden, E., Narain, U., Ray, D., Ruta, G., West, P. C., Wolny, S., Zaveri, E., & Damania, R.** (2026). *Landscape efficiency frontiers for biodiversity, climate mitigation, and net economic value*. **Science**, 392(6802), 1069–1074. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aea9058

  **Summary:** This study asks whether countries must trade off biodiversity conservation, climate mitigation, and economic development when deciding how to use land, or whether all three can be improved together. By combining spatial biophysical and economic data with optimization models across 146 countries, the authors map "landscape efficiency frontiers" — the best achievable combinations of carbon storage, biodiversity, and net economic value from agriculture, livestock, and forestry — and find that most countries currently operate well inside their frontier, leaving large gains on the table. Reallocating land (restoring forests on highly productive land while intensifying crop yields elsewhere, especially in lower-income countries with currently low-yield farming) could increase global climate mitigation by over 200 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent (a 20%+ increase) or raise net economic value by over $350 billion (an 80%+ increase), without sacrificing biodiversity or agricultural output. The findings matter because they challenge the assumption that conservation and economic growth are inherently at odds, offering governments a data-driven path to align land-use policy with climate, biodiversity, and development goals simultaneously.
