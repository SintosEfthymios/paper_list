# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Bastien-Olvera, B. A., Aburto-Oropeza, O., Brander, L. M., Cheung, W. W. L., Emmerling, J., Free, C. M., Granella, F., Tavoni, M., Verschuur, J., & Ricke, K.** (2026). *Accounting for ocean impacts nearly doubles the social cost of carbon*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16(3), 297–304. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-025-02533-5

  **Summary:** This study couples biophysical models of coral reefs, fisheries, mangroves, and coastal infrastructure with a climate-economy model to estimate how damage to ocean ecosystems contributes to the social cost of carbon — the monetized harm caused by each additional tonne of CO2 emitted. The authors find that including ocean impacts nearly doubles the social cost of carbon, from about $51 to roughly $97 per tonne of CO2, driven by lost fisheries productivity, coral reef collapse, and coastal infrastructure damage from sea-level rise and storms, together worth nearly $2 trillion annually worldwide. This matters because most carbon-pricing frameworks and integrated assessment models have historically ignored ocean ecosystems, meaning real-world carbon prices — including major markets like the EU's, around €70–80 per tonne — substantially understate the true economic cost of emissions. By making these previously invisible ocean damages explicit and monetized, the study strengthens the case for higher carbon prices and more ambitious climate policy, and offers a template for incorporating overlooked natural-capital losses into future social-cost-of-carbon estimates.
