# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Vandyck, T., Temursho, U., Landis, F., Klenert, D., & Weitzel, M.** (2026). *Enhancing fairness in climate action through policy packages*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16, 1273–1278. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02751-5

  **Summary:** This study asks how the EU can cut household CO2 emissions without placing unfair burdens on particular groups, evaluating the distributional effects of alternative climate policy packages across European households. The authors distinguish two kinds of fairness: vertical equity (impacts across income groups) and horizontal equity (differing impacts among households with similar incomes, e.g. by location, heating system or car dependence). They find that relying on any single approach forces a trade-off: carbon pricing with uniform cash transfers protects lower-income households on average but leaves large variation within income groups, while regulatory standards reduce that within-group variation but are less progressive. A mixed package — carbon prices whose revenues fund uniform transfers and energy-efficiency subsidies, combined with standards — balances both dimensions better than either tax-and-transfer or regulation-only designs. The results give a quantified, equity-based rationale for comprehensive policy packages, which is timely as the EU's ETS2 starts pricing household building and transport emissions in 2028 and member states design their Social Climate Plans.
