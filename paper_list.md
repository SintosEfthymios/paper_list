# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Pan, G., Kornek, U., Gong, C. C., Molina Bacca, E. J., Luderer, G., & Edenhofer, O.** (2026). *Quantifying assets for the geopolitics of climate change mitigation*. **Nature Sustainability**. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-026-01971-z

  **Summary:** This study asks how climate mitigation changes the economic assets of nations, and what that means for whose interests lie with or against stronger global climate action. Combining integrated assessment models, climate projections, damage functions and biosphere data, the authors quantify four climate-related asset types for 169 countries under mitigation: fossil fuel production value, avoided climate damages, renewable energy potential, and land carbon sequestration capacity. They find these gains and losses are very unevenly distributed. About 19 fossil-fuel-dependent countries, together producing roughly 6% of world output, are most exposed to lost revenue. Many Global South countries, especially in Africa, gain the most from avoided damages and solar potential, yet have the least economic weight. Major powers (the US, EU and China) turn out to be affected in surprisingly similar and relatively modest ways. The findings give an asset-based map of national interests in climate diplomacy, and point to "package deals" as a way to build cooperation where those interests diverge.
