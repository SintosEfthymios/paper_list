# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Choi, J., Jing, R., Callahan, C. W., Burke, M., & Diffenbaugh, N. S.** (2026). *The debt burden of tropical cyclones and climate change*. **Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS)**, 123(47). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2603924123

  **Summary:** This study traces how tropical cyclones and rising temperatures have changed national public finances. It combines historical storm and temperature impacts on economic growth (1980–2019) with a machine-learning model of sovereign credit ratings and borrowing costs, covering 181 countries, including many hot or storm-exposed countries that have no bond-market data. The authors find that tropical cyclones account for about 20% of the debt-to-GDP burden in cyclone-exposed countries. Combined cyclone and warming impacts have left GDP about 12% lower on average across all countries. These effects already add persistent credit-risk premiums of at least 1 basis point in 64 countries, and 5 or more basis points in highly exposed ones. Hotter countries become more likely to fall below investment grade, while some colder, wealthier countries may see upgrades. The results suggest climate change is already raising the cost of capital for the countries that most need affordable finance to adapt, so the authors argue climate risk should be built into debt relief, credit ratings, and climate-finance design.
