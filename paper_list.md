# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Middelanis, R., Jafino, B. A., & Hallegatte, S.** (2026). *Global socio-economic resilience to disasters*. **Nature Sustainability**. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-026-01947-z

  **Summary:** This study estimates how natural disasters (floods, storms, earthquakes and similar hazards) affect human well-being in 132 countries, not just the dollar value of buildings and infrastructure destroyed. Using household-level microsimulations, the authors find that global well-being losses exceed US$1 trillion a year, roughly double the conventional asset-damage figures. The losses fall unevenly: the poorest fifth of each country bears only 9% of asset losses but 33% of well-being losses, and low-income countries suffer 67% more well-being loss per dollar of damage and take 56% longer to recover than high-income countries. The work matters because it shows that asset-based risk metrics systematically understate disaster impacts on the poor, and that social protection, insurance and other financial tools aimed at low-income households can deliver larger well-being gains than infrastructure-focused policies alone.
