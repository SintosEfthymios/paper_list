# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Dureti, G., Hadi, H., & Wuepper, D.** (2026). *Agri-environmental policies have reduced cropland degradation globally*. **Nature Food**, 7, 527–538. https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-026-01359-4

  **Summary:** This study provides the first global, causal evidence that national agri-environmental policies actually improve cropland condition, built on a dataset linking roughly 4,700 national policies to 83 million satellite-based measurements of cropland health worldwide from 2001 to 2019. To isolate policy effects from confounding factors like climate and farming practices, the authors use a natural-experiment design comparing cropland condition just across international borders, where one country introduced a new safeguard and its neighbor did not. They find that agri-environmental policies improved cropland condition globally by roughly 2–5% on average — with payments to farmers and soil/land-use regulations the most effective instruments — though country-level effects range from 0 to over 20% depending on institutional quality and policy budgets. The findings matter because cropland degradation threatens food security and carbon storage for billions of people, and this is the first large-scale causal demonstration that policy, not just good intentions, can measurably slow or reverse it, offering evidence-based guidance on which instruments governments should prioritize.
