# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Rising, J., Khurana, R., Dietz, S., Dumas, M., Kikstra, J., Lenton, T. M., Linsenmeier, M., Smith, C., & Ward, B.** (2026). *Comprehensive national climate damage assessments framework applied to the UK*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16, 790–795. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02665-2

  **Summary:** This study introduces the most comprehensive national-scale economic damage assessment produced to date, an emulation framework that estimates climate risks channel-by-channel (health, labor productivity, agriculture, catastrophic risks, cross-border spillovers, and other previously "missing" risks) and disaggregates results across 406 local areas of the UK. The authors find that today's roughly 1.5°C of warming is already costing the UK economy and welfare the equivalent of about 2% of GDP (95% CI: 1-4%), rising rapidly to around 10% of GDP (2-20%) by 2100 under a baseline warming trajectory, and to as much as 20% of GDP if warming reaches 4°C; accounting for the disproportionate toll on poorer communities pushes the central total welfare loss estimate to about 15% of GDP. This matters because it shows that conventional, narrower national climate-damage estimates have substantially understated both the economic and human costs of climate change, with direct implications for how governments value adaptation spending and set climate policy targets.
