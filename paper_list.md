# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Dureti, G. G., Hadi, H., & Wuepper, D.** (2026). *Agri-environmental policies have reduced cropland degradation globally*. **Nature Food**, 7, 527–538. https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-026-01359-4

  **Summary:** This study provides the first global, causal evidence on whether national agri-environmental policies actually improve farmland, using ~83 million satellite-based measurements of cropland condition from 2001–2019 linked to roughly 4,700 national policies across the world. Applying two complementary quasi-experimental methods (difference-in-discontinuities and difference-in-differences), the authors find that these policies have reduced cropland degradation and improved land condition globally by an estimated 2–5% on average, though effects range from essentially zero to over 20% depending on the country. Agri-environmental payments and soil/land-use regulations proved most effective, while input and habitat regulations showed weak effects, with the variation explained largely by countries' governance quality and policy budgets. The findings matter for global food security and climate policy because they show, at unprecedented scale, that well-designed agri-environmental policy can measurably slow the degradation of the soils that underpin the world's food supply — and point to which policy instruments and institutional conditions make that support effective.
