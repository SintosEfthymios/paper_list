# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Agarwala, M., Burke, M., Klusak, P., Kraemer, M., Volz, U., & Sovacool, B. K.** (2026). *Biodiversity loss will decrease the future creditworthiness of nations*. **Nature Ecology & Evolution**, 10(8), 1511–1520. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-026-03081-7

  **Summary:** This study is the first to build biodiversity and ecosystem-service loss directly into sovereign credit risk analysis, extending S&P Global's rating methodology to model how declining tropical timber stocks, wild pollination, and marine fisheries would affect 23 countries home to 5.5 billion people. The authors validate their approach against a real case — Madagascar's 2022 downgrade — and then project forward: under a scenario of partial ecosystem collapse, annual government debt-servicing costs would rise by an estimated $70 billion in China and $49 billion in India, with additional interest payments across all studied countries reaching roughly $162 billion a year. Because current sovereign credit ratings ignore these nature-related risks entirely, the authors argue that markets are mispricing, mismanaging, and misallocating some $83 trillion in financial assets. The findings matter because they connect biodiversity conservation directly to the cost of government borrowing, giving finance ministries and credit-rating agencies a concrete economic reason — beyond ecological or ethical ones — to treat nature loss as a fiscal risk.
