# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Waidelich, P., Crassier, C., de Boer, H. S., Tautorat, P., van Vuuren, D. P., & Steffen, B.** (2026). *The impact of financing cost differences on global energy and industry decarbonization*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16, 1178–1186. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02756-0

  **Summary:** Integrated assessment models that underpin global climate scenarios usually assume one uniform cost of capital everywhere, ignoring the much higher borrowing costs faced by investors in developing countries. The authors estimated country- and sector-specific costs of capital for eight energy and industry sectors (including power grids, hydrogen, steel and cement) and built them into the IMAGE model. Accounting for developing countries' higher investment risks raises global mitigation costs through 2100 for well-below-2 °C pathways by about 9% compared with a uniform low-risk assumption, shifts abatement toward lower-risk regions, reduces grid investment in developing countries, tilts centralized heat toward natural gas, raises green-hydrogen costs, and holds back capital-intensive carbon capture and electrified steel and cement. The results show that financing conditions — not just technology costs — shape where and how fast decarbonization can happen, strengthening the case for climate finance, guarantees and insurance from high-income countries across many sectors, not only renewable power.
