# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Rising, J., Khurana, R., Dietz, S., Dumas, M., Kikstra, J., Lenton, T. M., Linsenmeier, M., Smith, C., & Ward, B.** (2026). *Comprehensive national climate damage assessments framework applied to the UK*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16(7). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02665-2

  **Summary:** This paper develops a new "emulation" methodology for comprehensive national climate damage assessment, built to overcome the fragmented and incomplete nature of existing economic climate-impact estimates. Applying it to the UK, the authors combine channel-specific damage estimates (health, productivity, agriculture, and more) with explicit treatment of climatic and socioeconomic uncertainty, disaggregating results to 406 local areas and — crucially — including often-omitted risks such as cross-border spillovers and catastrophic or "missing" risks. They find that current warming already produces welfare losses equivalent to about 2% of GDP (95% CI 1–4%), a figure that grows rapidly under baseline warming to roughly 10% of GDP (2–20%) by 2100, with roughly half of expected damages and most of the uncertainty driven by catastrophic and missing risks. The work matters because it offers a replicable template for national governments to quantify climate risk far more completely than prior single-channel or highly aggregated approaches, with direct relevance for national adaptation planning and climate risk disclosure.
