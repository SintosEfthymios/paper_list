# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Polasky, S., Hawthorne, P. L., Chaplin-Kramer, R., Smith, J., Gerber, J. S., Mamun, S., Ruckelshaus, M., Russ, J., Schmitt, R., Vogl, A. L., Castonguay, A. C., Douglass, J., Kowal, V., Madden, I., Sharp, R., Sohngen, B., Chang, J., Daily, G., & Damania, R.** (2026). *Landscape efficiency frontiers for biodiversity, climate mitigation, and net economic value*. **Science**, 392(6802), 1069–1074. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aea9058

  **Summary:** This study asks whether countries must trade off biodiversity conservation, climate mitigation, and economic value from agriculture, livestock, and forestry, or whether better land use and management could improve all three at once. By combining spatial biophysical and economic data with optimization methods across 146 countries, the authors compute "landscape efficiency frontiers" — the maximum feasible combinations of the three objectives — and compare them to current land-use outcomes. They find that most countries currently sit well inside their frontier, meaning smarter land allocation and management could jointly deliver more than 200 billion additional tonnes of CO2-equivalent climate mitigation (over 20% more) and upward of $350 billion more net economic value (over 80% more), without sacrificing biodiversity or the other goals. This challenges the common assumption that environmental protection necessarily costs economic growth, and offers policymakers a data-driven roadmap for land-use reforms that could simultaneously advance climate, biodiversity, and development targets.
