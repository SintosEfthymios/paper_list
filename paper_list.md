# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Dureti, G., Hadi, H., & Wuepper, D.** (2026). *Agri-environmental policies have reduced cropland degradation globally*. **Nature Food**, 7, 527–538. https://doi.org/10.1038/s43016-026-01359-4

  **Summary:** This study provides the first large-scale causal evidence on whether agri-environmental policies actually work, linking a global dataset of roughly 4,700 national policies to 83 million satellite-based observations of cropland condition from 2001 to 2019. Using quasi-experimental comparisons — including neighboring countries where only one side adopted a new safeguard — the authors estimate that these policies have improved global cropland condition by at least 2% and possibly up to 5% on average, though effects range from essentially zero to over 20% depending on the country. Agri-environmental payments and soil/land-use regulations were the most effective policy types, while input and habitat regulations showed weak effects, and a country's institutional quality and policy budget strongly shaped outcomes. The findings matter because they give policymakers the first robust global evidence that agri-environmental policy can meaningfully slow cropland degradation — a key input into long-term food security and climate resilience — while also showing which policy designs and governance conditions actually deliver results.
