# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Noack, F., Rohner, D., & Sonno, T.** (2026). *The environmental impact of multinational firms in Africa*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16, 674–680. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02637-6

  **Summary:** This study provides continent-wide causal evidence on how multinational corporate investment affects the environment, combining firm-level economic data with satellite measurements of deforestation, crop diversity, and emissions across Africa from 2007 to 2018. The authors find that areas with greater multinational activity experienced about 24% more deforestation and a 0.6% decline in local crop diversity than areas with only domestic firms, with each additional multinational affiliate associated with roughly 10,200 hectares of forest loss. While each new affiliate raised local GDP by about 0.3% (an estimated $106 million), the researchers value the resulting carbon and environmental damages at around $693 million per affiliate — several times larger than the economic gain, and driven in part by weak host-country governance. The findings challenge the assumption that foreign investment is environmentally neutral relative to domestic activity and carry direct implications for how home countries and international investors should regulate the environmental footprint of multinational firms operating abroad.
