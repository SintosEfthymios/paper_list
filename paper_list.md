# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Bastien-Olvera, B. A., Aburto-Oropeza, O., Brander, L. M., Cheung, W. W. L., Emmerling, J., Free, C. M., Granella, F., Tavoni, M., Verschuur, J., & Ricke, K.** (2026). *Accounting for ocean impacts nearly doubles the social cost of carbon*. **Nature Climate Change**, 16, 297–304. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-025-02533-5

  **Summary:** This study is the first to integrate ocean-related climate damages — to coral reefs, mangroves, seaports, fisheries, and mariculture — into a standard climate-economy model used to compute the social cost of carbon (SCC), the standard dollar measure of harm caused by each tonne of CO2 emitted. The authors find that this "blue" SCC comes to about $48 per tonne of CO2 in 2020 (versus roughly $25 without ocean impacts), nearly doubling conventional estimates, with global ocean-related damages reaching an estimated $2 trillion annually through losses like coastal protection, fisheries collapse, and reduced seafood-based nutrition. These harms fall disproportionately on islands and coastal developing economies that depend heavily on the ocean for food and livelihoods. The findings matter because they show that widely used climate policy benchmarks have been significantly underpricing carbon by omitting the ocean, with direct implications for climate regulation, carbon pricing, and investment in marine ecosystem restoration and adaptation.
