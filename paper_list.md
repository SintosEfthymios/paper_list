# Paper List

A running list of notable papers, with brief summaries.

## Environmental / Economic Science

- **Burke, M., Zahid, M., Diffenbaugh, N. S., & Hsiang, S.** (2026). *Quantifying climate loss and damage consistent with a social cost of carbon*. **Nature**, 651, 959–966. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10272-6

  **Summary:** This study builds the first quantitative framework linking specific past and future CO2 emissions to monetized, location-specific climate damages, tying the accounting directly to the social cost of carbon. Using empirical estimates of the non-linear relationship between temperature and aggregate economic output, the authors find that the bulk of the economic damage from any given tonne of CO2 is still to come: a tonne emitted in 1990 caused about $180 in discounted global damages by 2020, but is projected to cause an additional $1,840 through 2100 — roughly ten times more. This means historical "loss and damage" accounting based only on harm already incurred vastly understates the true liability of past emissions. The findings matter for international climate finance and loss-and-damage negotiations, since they suggest far larger future costs are already locked in from emissions released decades ago, with direct implications for how climate liability and reparations should be calculated going forward.

- **Moore, F. C., Haqiqi, I., Kong, Q., Rennels, L., Baldos, U., Ganapathi, H., Huber, M., & Hertel, T.** (2026). *New labour and agricultural damages improve climate cost estimates*. **Nature Climate Change** (published online September 2026). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41558-026-02749-z

  **Summary:** This study revises two key parts of the social cost of carbon (SCC), the estimate of the damage caused by each extra tonne of CO2 that U.S. policy uses: losses in labour productivity and losses in agriculture. The authors combine bias-corrected projections of wet-bulb globe temperature, a measure of heat stress, with estimates of how heat affects labour in specific occupations and sectors. They find that heat-related lost labour is a major cost the SCC has mostly left out. Press coverage reports about $41 in lost productivity per tonne of CO2 emitted in 2025, which makes labour the second-largest part of the SCC after heat-related deaths. The losses fall hardest on India, Nigeria, China, Pakistan, the Middle East and sub-Saharan Africa. Using evidence from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report, the authors also lower the estimate of agricultural damages from US$95 to US$29 per tonne. The work matters because the SCC is used to weigh the costs and benefits of climate regulation. Correcting it changes which climate harms count as most important for policy: labour moves up and agriculture moves down.
